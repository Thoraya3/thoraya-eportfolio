## What? (Description)

In Unit 9 of Numerical Analysis, I studied cross-tabulations, contingency tables, subsampling techniques, and Chi-Square analysis using R. The unit focused on understanding relationships between categorical variables and how statistical methods can be used to determine whether associations exist between variables within a dataset.

<br>

The lecture materials introduced cross-tabulation as a method for organising and presenting frequency data. I learned how contingency tables can be used to explore relationships between categorical variables and how row percentages, column percentages, and frequency counts support data interpretation.

<br>

The unit also introduced the Chi-Square Test of Independence, one of the most widely used nonparametric tests for categorical data analysis. I learned how Chi-Square analysis compares observed frequencies with expected frequencies and how p-values are used to determine whether variables are statistically associated.

<br>

As part of the practical activities, I completed Data Activity 7 using the COVID-19 India Dataset. The activity involved creating cross-tabulations to analyse the relationship between Region and Case Severity Level, and between Case Severity Level and Recovery Status.

<br>

I also participated in Collaborative Discussion 2, which focused on the misinterpretation of statistical information, confidence intervals, and p-values in statistical reporting and decision-making.

## Collaborative Discussion 2 – Misinterpretation of Statistical Information

<br>

This discussion focused on the misuse and misinterpretation of confidence intervals and p-values within statistical reporting.

<br>

The discussion was based on the work of Greenland et al. (2016) and Tijssen (2021) and explored the following questions:

<br>

1. Under what situations should confidence intervals not be reported?
2. Why is a p-value not always enough to report?

<br>

### Initial Post

<br>

In my initial discussion contribution, I explored the limitations of confidence intervals and p-values. I discussed how confidence intervals depend upon assumptions related to sampling procedures, study design, and statistical models. When these assumptions are violated, confidence intervals may provide misleading information.

<br>

I also explained that p-values should not be interpreted as standalone evidence. Statistical significance does not automatically imply practical significance, and researchers should also consider effect size, confidence intervals, methodology, and context when evaluating results.

<br>

### Peer Response 1

<br>

I responded to a peer who discussed the importance of understanding the assumptions underlying confidence intervals and statistical models.

<br>

My response emphasised the need to report confidence intervals, p-values, and effect sizes together in order to communicate uncertainty, precision, and practical significance effectively.

<br>

### Peer Response 2

<br>

I responded to a peer who discussed the limitations of relying on the traditional 0.05 significance threshold and highlighted issues such as p-hacking and selective reporting.

<br>

My response reinforced the importance of considering confidence intervals, effect sizes, and research quality rather than focusing solely on statistical significance.

<br>

### Key Learning Points

<br>

Through this discussion, I learned that:

- Confidence intervals depend on statistical assumptions and study design.
- P-values should not be interpreted independently.
- Statistical significance does not necessarily imply practical significance.
- Effect size is an important component of statistical reporting.
- Critical evaluation is essential when interpreting statistical findings.

<br>

The discussion strengthened my ability to critically evaluate published research and improved my understanding of responsible statistical reporting.
